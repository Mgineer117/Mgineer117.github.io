There is a complaint you hear in almost every department, usually late at night
and usually from someone two years in: *my advisor doesn't know anything about
my literature, so I can't learn anything from them.* I understand where it comes
from. I also think it rests on a reading of the word **learn** that doesn't
survive contact with what a PhD actually is.

## Your advisor is not supposed to know your problem

Consider what a professor's own PhD looked like. It went deep — down into one
thesis, one set of questions, one corner of one field. A postdoc goes deeper
still. That depth is the whole point of the degree, and it is bought at the cost
of breadth.

Now consider the research you are doing. If your advisor is a junior professor,
their thesis is close enough in time that people expect it to overlap with your
work. Often it doesn't. You are solving a problem that sits somewhere else, and
even when it sits nearby, the specific machinery you are using is machinery you
picked up and they did not. So it is entirely natural that they cannot follow
every technical step, and cannot hand you a verdict on the derivation you spent
last week on.

That is not a failure of the advisor. In a healthy group it's a sign the student
has gone somewhere the advisor hasn't. Which is, eventually, the objective.

## Then what is the student's job?

Three things, and I think they are all on the student.

**Understand the situation for what it is.** If you are waiting for someone to
check your work line by line, you will wait for years and grow resentful about
it. Nobody is coming. The sooner that lands, the sooner the relationship gets
useful.

**Ask the kind of question your advisor can actually answer.** This is the part
I would most want a first-year to hear, because it is a small change in phrasing
that changes everything you get back.

Don't ask:

> Is A correct?

Ask:

> To do A, I did B. Does this approach make sense?

The first question outsources a verdict. It asks someone to be the referee on
technical content that, as established above, they may not hold at your depth —
so the honest answer is *I don't know*, and you leave with nothing. The second
question asks for judgment about direction: is this a reasonable way to attack
the problem, is it the kind of thing that has worked before, is there a cheaper
route, is this even the problem worth attacking. That is a question about
research taste, and research taste is exactly what your advisor has more of than
you do. It is also the thing that does not go stale — it transfers across fields
in a way that technical detail does not.

Ask for that, repeatedly, and you will find the well is much deeper than you
thought.

**Remember the definition of the degree.** A PhD is, by definition, learning to
conduct research on your own. Not learning a subject — you can do that from
books and papers, and you will. The advisor's role is not to be your senior
engineer. It is to guide you into a position where you can carry a research
problem by yourself: choose it, frame it, judge whether it's going anywhere, and
know when to stop.

Measured against that role, an advisor not having your technical depth is not a
defect. It is close to the intended end state. If they still had it in year
four, one of you hasn't moved.

## The relationship this produces

None of this is an argument for expecting less. It is an argument for asking for
the right thing, and it tends to produce a much better relationship than the
alternative. You stop arriving at meetings with a list of things to be graded on
and start arriving with a position: here is what I tried, here is why, here is
where I think it goes, tell me where I'm wrong about the *direction*. Your
advisor gets to do the thing they are good at. You get to own the work.

And you come out the other end with the thing the degree was for, which was
never a set of answers.
